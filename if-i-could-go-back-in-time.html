If I could go back in time…

What I wish I’d understood before my children started high school—and what you can do now.

By Christopher Rogers

View the student brochure and download the PDF →

I have two children who are now seniors in high school. Like many other parents, I'm preparing to make a significant financial investment in their future. But the stakes are about much more than money. I want my children to find a college where they'll grow, mature, discover what interests them, and begin preparing for meaningful work and lives of their own.

And I'm realizing, perhaps a little late, how much of the work that shapes those opportunities happens long before senior year.

I haven't been particularly involved in their academic planning. I've logged many hours in the evening sitting alongside my kids while they've worked. But the role I've played has largely been helping them get assignments done on time. I haven't participated in conversations with their college and career counselor. I've assumed that the school, my children, and the process would take care of things.

Now, as applications approach, I'm discovering that some of the most important decisions have already been made.

Which raises an uncomfortable question: If a reasonably engaged parent like me can arrive at senior year feeling this unprepared, what does that say about the system we've built to help families navigate college?

We've been starting in the wrong place

American college admissions culture seems organized around one dominant question: What's the best college I can get into?

We have rankings to tell us which colleges are best. Admissions statistics to tell us how competitive they are. Calculators to estimate our chances. Checklists to keep applications on track. And, for families with the resources, private consultants who can help manage the process.

Much of this is genuinely useful. But the collective effect is to focus our attention on the destination, often before we've thought carefully about the student making the journey.

I've come to think we should reverse the process.

Stop starting with the college. Start with the student.

What interests them? What are they good at? Where might they thrive? What kinds of learning experiences excite them? What environments help them grow?

Then work backward.

Which schools and programs might be a good fit? What does it take to become a competitive applicant? What academic choices and experiences could help? And, perhaps most importantly, what should they be doing now, while there's still time to make a difference?

That's a fundamentally different approach to college planning.

This changes the starting point and the work that follows: understand the student, identify several places they might thrive, and work backward to the choices available today.

It's the difference between reacting to admissions requirements in senior year and making informed choices throughout high school. Between chasing prestige and developing a sense of fit. Between hoping to get in somewhere good and deliberately preparing for a range of places where you could succeed.

And it doesn't require a fourteen-year-old to know what they want to do with their life.

Quite the opposite.

Start broad. Narrow thoughtfully. Keep options open.

If I could go back to ninth grade, I wouldn't ask my children to identify their dream college. I'd ask them to start exploring.

Do they imagine themselves in a big city or a small town? Do they prefer hands-on projects or more traditional classroom instruction? Do they enjoy competitive environments or find them exhausting? What subjects spark their curiosity? What kinds of problems do they like solving?

Some of those questions might seem premature. Others might seem unrelated to college admissions.

But I'd argue they're exactly the right place to begin.

I'd approach college planning as an evolving portfolio of possibilities. Start with a broad collection of interests, environments, programs, and schools. Over time, explore them, test assumptions, and narrow the field as the student learns more about themselves.

Not a funnel that ends with one perfect college. A portfolio that becomes more informed and intentional over time.

A student might begin ninth grade interested in science, discover a passion for design in tenth grade, and find themselves drawn to engineering by eleventh. Another might start with a particular career in mind and discover that the kind of learning environment matters more to them than the major.

That's not indecision. That's discovery.

But here's the part I wish I'd understood earlier: exploration and preparation need to happen together.

A student can keep their options open intellectually while unintentionally closing them academically.

Course selection, academic rigor, prerequisites, and extracurricular experiences can all influence which opportunities remain available later. According to the National Association for College Admission Counseling’s Fall 2023 admission survey, grades in college-preparatory courses, overall grades, and curriculum strength were among the factors most frequently rated as considerably important in admissions decisions.

A student who discovers an interest in engineering during junior year may find that earlier math choices have made some programs harder to pursue. That doesn't mean the opportunity is gone. But it might mean a more difficult path.

I'd want my children to understand those connections earlier—not to pressure them into choosing a destination, but to preserve their ability to choose one later.

The counselor's dilemma

Recently, I spoke with the principal of my children's high school about the challenges facing public education. We talked about budgets, resource constraints, and the difficult trade-offs administrators make every day.

Then I asked a different question.

If she had more resources, what would she invest in?

One of her answers has stayed with me.

She described an exceptional college and career counselor at the school. Someone who makes a real difference for students. Given a magic wand, she'd replicate that person so every student could receive sustained, one-on-one guidance beginning in ninth grade.

That struck me.

Her aspiration wasn't another college-search platform or application checklist. It was more access to a person who already knew how to help.

And the scale of the challenge is significant.

According to the American School Counselor Association, the national student-to-school-counselor ratio was 372 to 1 in 2024–25, across all grade levels. The association recommends 250 to 1.

Those figures don't tell us how much time an individual high school counselor spends with students, or how effective that guidance is. But they do illustrate the capacity problem.

Imagine maintaining meaningful, individualized relationships with hundreds of students, each with different interests, ambitions, academic circumstances, and family situations.

Now imagine trying to help each of them develop and continuously refine a four-year plan.

It's a lot to ask.

And it made me think differently about the role parents could play.

What I'd do differently as a parent

Looking back, I don't think I needed to spend more evenings supervising homework.

I needed to spend more time helping my children understand where their education might take them.

That's an important distinction.

I don't want to become their college counselor. I don't want to manage every decision, impose my ambitions on them, or turn high school into a four-year college admissions campaign.

But I could have played a more useful role.

If I could start again, I'd approach it this way:

For students: five habits I’d encourage

  1. Explore deliberately. Don't wait until application season to discover what kinds of schools, programs, and experiences appeal to you. Follow your curiosity. Visit different environments. Ask questions. Change your mind.
  2. Keep a portfolio of possibilities. Maintain several promising directions rather than committing too early to one school or career. Let the portfolio evolve as your interests and understanding grow.
  3. Understand what preparation looks like. For schools and programs that interest you, investigate what makes an applicant competitive. Look beyond minimum requirements to coursework, academic preparation, and relevant experiences.
  4. Make a plan, then revisit it. Identify a few meaningful actions each semester. Understand why they matter. Reassess as your interests, performance, and circumstances change.
  5. Use your counselor as a partner. Arrive with questions, ideas, and a developing plan. Ask for advice on trade-offs, opportunities, and blind spots. Make the most of the time you have together.

For parents: five commitments I’d make

  1. Understand the academic plan. Know what courses my children are taking, what choices are coming, and how those decisions might affect future opportunities.
  2. Have regular, low-pressure conversations. Not weekly interrogations about college, but a few purposeful conversations each semester about interests, possibilities, and next steps.
  3. Help make opportunities visible. Bring forward programs, experiences, and schools they might not otherwise discover. Help them explore without deciding for them.
  4. Support the counselor relationship. Understand the counselor's role, encourage my children to engage early, and help them prepare thoughtful questions. Participate appropriately, while keeping the student at the center.
  5. Focus on progress, not prestige. Measure success by growing clarity, preparation, confidence, and meaningful options—not simply by the ranking of the schools on an application list.

None of this requires parents to become admissions experts.

It does require us to be more intentional about when and how we engage.

And I suspect it could make the counselor's limited time more productive, too.

Working with your counselor

Imagine a student arriving at a counseling appointment with a developing portfolio of interests and possible schools, a clear picture of their academic preparation, and two or three questions about what to do next.

That's a different conversation from asking, for the first time in senior year, where they should apply.

Making better guidance possible

All of this has led me to explore a question: Could we make personalized college planning more accessible without requiring more hours from already stretched counselors or turning parents into full-time college advisors?

I'm working on an AI-assisted concept called Project Jarvis to investigate that possibility.

The idea is relatively straightforward. Help students explore schools and programs that might fit, understand how their current preparation compares with the expectations of those programs, and develop an evolving plan of practical next steps.

Give parents enough visibility to be helpful without taking over. Give counselors a more informed starting point for the conversations where their expertise matters most.

And make the experience continuous rather than something students scramble to assemble in junior or senior year.

There are already products addressing parts of this problem. Whether Jarvis can offer something meaningfully better remains to be tested. And no tool can guarantee admission or predict where a student will thrive.

But the larger opportunity seems worth pursuing.

We have an enormous amount of information about colleges. What families need is help turning that information into understanding, decisions, and action—early enough for those actions to matter.

Looking north

I can't go back to ninth grade with my own children.

And I don't want to suggest that their opportunities have been lost, or that we haven't made good decisions along the way. They're remarkable young people with futures full of possibility.

But I do wish I'd approached these years with a clearer sense of what mattered.

Less emphasis on completing today's assignments. More attention to how today's choices shape tomorrow's options.

Less fascination with rankings. More curiosity about fit.

Less waiting until college applications became urgent. More deliberate exploration and preparation along the way.

My hope is that other parents can benefit from what I'm learning, and perhaps begin some of these conversations earlier than I did.

Because the goal was never simply to get our children into the best college they could.

It was to help them discover where they might thrive—and give them the preparation, confidence, and freedom to get there.
