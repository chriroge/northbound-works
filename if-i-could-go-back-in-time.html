College Planning: A Parent’s Perspective

A parent’s reflection on starting college planning earlier, helping families connect students’ interests with practical steps and informed choices.

By Christopher Rogers

View the student brochure and download the PDF →

My two children are now high school seniors. I want them to find colleges where they can grow, discover what interests them, and prepare for meaningful work and lives of their own. I’m realizing how much of that preparation begins long before senior year.

I’ve spent many evenings helping them finish assignments, but much less time helping them plan their education. I haven’t participated in conversations with their college and career counselor. I assumed the school, my children, and the process would take care of things. As applications approach, I’m discovering that some important choices have already been made.

Start with the student

College rankings, admissions statistics, and application checklists can be useful. But I’ve found it easy to focus on getting into a highly ranked school before asking where my children might thrive.

I’d begin with their interests, strengths, and learning preferences. Then I’d help them explore several schools and programs that might fit, and understand what courses and experiences would prepare them.

That gives families something useful to do throughout high school. It doesn’t require a fourteen-year-old to choose a career or a single dream college.

Explore interests and prepare together

If I could start ninth grade again with my children, I’d ask what they wanted to explore. A city or a small town? Hands-on projects or classroom discussion? Which subjects and problems made them curious?

I’d keep a changing list of interests, environments, programs, and schools. A student might discover an interest in design after starting with science, or learn that the setting matters more than the major. Those discoveries should help shape the plan.

Exploration and preparation need to happen together. Course choices and prerequisites can affect later options. According to the National Association for College Admission Counseling’s Fall 2023 admission survey, grades in college-preparatory courses, overall grades, and curriculum strength were among the factors most frequently rated as considerably important in admissions decisions.

A student who discovers engineering in junior year may find that earlier math choices make some programs harder to pursue. I’d want my children to understand those connections early enough to make informed choices.

Make more of the guidance available

When I asked my children’s high school principal what she would do with more resources, she described an exceptional college and career counselor. Given a magic wand, she’d replicate that person so every student could have sustained, individual guidance from ninth grade.

According to the American School Counselor Association, the national student-to-school-counselor ratio was 372 to 1 in 2024–25, across all grade levels. ASCA recommends 250 to 1. Those figures don’t measure the time or quality of guidance at a particular high school, but they illustrate the demands on counselors.

That conversation made me think about how parents can help students prepare for the time they do have with their counselor.

What I would do differently

I don’t need to become my children’s college counselor or manage every decision. I can help them understand their options, ask useful questions, and prepare for choices before they become urgent.

For students: five habits I’d encourage

  1. Explore early. Try subjects, activities, and environments that interest you. Ask questions and allow yourself to change your mind.
  2. Keep several possibilities open. Maintain a list of promising programs, schools, and experiences, including why each appeals to you.
  3. Understand the preparation. Ask about courses, prerequisites, and experiences that support the options you’re considering.
  4. Choose practical next steps. Plan a few useful actions each semester and revise them as you learn.
  5. Work with your counselor. Bring your ideas and questions, and ask what you might be overlooking.

For parents: five commitments I’d make

  1. Understand upcoming choices. Learn what courses my children are taking and how their next decisions might affect their options.
  2. Keep conversations low-pressure. Talk about interests and next steps a few times each semester.
  3. Make opportunities visible. Suggest experiences and programs they might not find themselves, without deciding for them.
  4. Support the counselor relationship. Help my children prepare questions and participate appropriately while respecting their decisions.
  5. Look beyond rankings. Pay attention to growing clarity, preparation, confidence, and meaningful choices.

Working with your counselor

Bring a few interests or possible schools, a clear picture of current courses and preparation, and two or three questions. That creates a more useful starting point than waiting until senior year to ask where to apply.

Exploring a planning tool

This experience led me to Project Jarvis, an early-stage AI-assisted concept for college planning. It explores how students, parents, and counselors could use a shared academic plan to connect college goals with practical next steps.

The aim is to give parents enough visibility to help and counselors a more informed starting point for conversations. Whether Jarvis can improve on existing tools remains to be tested. No tool can guarantee admission or predict where a student will thrive.

What I hope other families can use

I can’t go back to ninth grade with my children, and I don’t believe their opportunities have been lost. They’re remarkable young people with futures full of possibility.

I do wish I’d paid more attention to how today’s choices shape tomorrow’s options. My hope is that other families can begin these conversations earlier and help their children prepare for places where they might thrive.
